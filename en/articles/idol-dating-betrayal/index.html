Why Does Idol Dating Feel Like Betrayal?

The strongest explanation is not that fans confuse fantasy with reality. It is that ordinary systems for familiarity, expectation and identity can make a one-sided relationship psychologically meaningful—and make a sudden update feel personal.

Imagine that an idol you have followed for years announces a romantic relationship. You know, rationally, that you were never dating this person. You may even believe they deserve privacy and happiness. And yet the news can still produce a surprisingly physical mix of disappointment, jealousy, embarrassment, loss or anger.

Calling that reaction “delusional” is scientifically lazy. Calling it simply “parasocial” is not enough either. A better question is: what changed inside the fan’s social model when the news arrived?

The research does not support a single brain mechanism for “idol dating betrayal.” In fact, direct experimental evidence on idol dating announcements is still sparse. What we do have is a mature literature on parasocial relationships, a smaller literature on parasocial breakups, and growing evidence on K-pop fandom as a social identity. Put together carefully, these findings suggest a layered explanation: repeated exposure creates familiarity; mediated intimacy makes the person socially meaningful; expectations accumulate; fandom embeds the relationship inside a community; and unexpected romantic information can force several of those models to update at once.26

Evidence boundary. HUMAN treats “prediction error” and “model update” here as a mechanistic synthesis—not as a directly demonstrated neural signature of K-pop dating reactions.

01Parasocial does not mean fake.

The idea of a parasocial relationship is older than social media. Horton and Wohl introduced the concept in 1956 to describe the sense of intimacy audiences can develop with media figures who repeatedly appear to address them.1 The relationship is structurally asymmetric—the audience member knows far more about the performer than the performer knows about them—but asymmetry does not make the psychological representation empty.

Modern evidence makes an important correction to the stereotype that parasocial relationships are mainly substitutes for failed real-life relationships. A meta-analysis of 120 studies found no significant overall relationship between social deficiencies and parasocial relationships. Instead, stronger parasocial bonds were associated with many of the same ingredients that facilitate ordinary interpersonal bonds, including perceived similarity and other forms of media involvement.2

That matters because it changes the starting point. The relevant question is not “Why would a lonely person invent a relationship?” It is “How does a normal social system learn a person from repeated mediated information?”

02Digital media can make a one-sided bond unusually information-rich.

Traditional celebrity exposure was intermittent: a television appearance, an interview, an album, a magazine cover. Contemporary fandom can be continuous. A fan may encounter performances, livestreams, short-form clips, behind-the-scenes footage, direct-address messages and community discussion every day. The result is not reciprocity, but it is a dense stream of socially formatted information.

Research on Korean Wave fans illustrates why this matters. Chung and Cho studied 400 Korean Wave fans in Singapore and tested how social-media affordances, self-disclosure and parasocial relationships fit into celebrity–fan engagement.3 A separate study of 1,276 female fans of female celebrities found that perceived attractiveness and similarity predicted parasocial interaction, which in turn was positively related to fan-community identity and participation.7

None of this means that fans literally believe a celebrity is their friend or partner. It means the mind has enough repeated material to build a stable model: what this person sounds like, how they behave, what they value, what kinds of relationships seem possible, and how the fan expects the persona to continue.

03Dating news is not a breakup—but it can behave like a relationship-disrupting event.

The parasocial-breakup literature gives us the closest empirical analogue. Cohen developed measures of distress after the anticipated loss of a favorite media figure and later showed, in 381 adults, that people with stronger parasocial relationships anticipated more negative reactions when a favorite television character disappeared. Attachment style also mattered.54

More recent work reaches a similar conclusion in fictional media: stronger parasocial relationships predict greater emotional distress when a character relationship is disrupted or ends.8 These studies do not show that an idol dating announcement is psychologically identical to losing a character. But they demonstrate a key principle: changing access to, or expectations about, a parasocial figure can generate measurable loss-like responses.

The fan does not lose a real romantic relationship. They may lose a familiar model of what the relationship could mean.

This is where “prediction” becomes useful—if handled cautiously. Over time, the fan has learned regularities: the idol’s persona, availability, style of self-presentation, and the social scripts surrounding the fandom. Dating news can introduce information that is inconsistent with some of those expectations. The emotional response may therefore include the cost of revising a stable model, not only romantic jealousy.

What might be updating?

At least four things are plausible: the imagined possibility of special access; the coherence of the public persona; the fan’s assumptions about intimacy and exclusivity; and the expected social norms of the fandom. The relative importance of these components has not yet been cleanly decomposed in idol-dating experiments.

04The “relationship” is often larger than the idol and the fan.

Fandom is also a group identity. A large international study of 1,477 K-pop fans across 92 predominantly Western countries used social identity theory to examine how fanship related to psychosocial outcomes including social connectedness, self-esteem and happiness.6 The point is not that fandom automatically improves wellbeing. It is that being a fan can become part of how people categorize themselves and connect to others.

This changes the meaning of dating news. The event may happen between two public individuals, but the interpretation unfolds inside a network of fan accounts, group norms, jokes, rivalries, status hierarchies and shared histories. One fan may experience the news as irrelevant. Another may experience it as a change in a valued community narrative. A third may feel pressure to display the “correct” reaction to other fans.

That is why “parasocial jealousy” alone is too narrow. The same announcement can simultaneously touch a one-sided bond, a prediction about the idol, and a group identity.

05Why do some fans feel almost nothing?

If one mechanism caused the reaction, we would expect responses to look more uniform. They do not. Parasocial-breakup studies already show that relationship intensity and individual differences matter.4 Fandom studies also show wide variation in how strongly people identify with fan communities and how they use them socially.6

A useful model therefore treats the emotional response as weighted rather than binary. For one person, the strongest component may be romantic fantasy. For another, it may be surprise. For another, it may be concern about group conflict. For another, the news may simply update an irrelevant piece of celebrity information and disappear.

This variability is scientifically important because it argues against moralizing either side. Feeling disappointed does not automatically mean a fan is irrational or possessive. Feeling nothing does not mean the fan is less committed. Different psychological functions can sit underneath superficially similar fandom behavior.

06Emotion is not entitlement.

There is also a normative distinction that psychology should not blur: an emotion can arise automatically without justifying a behavior. Someone can feel jealousy and still respect a celebrity’s autonomy. Someone can feel loss and still recognize that no reciprocal promise was made.

This distinction is especially important in discussions of fandom because extreme behavior is highly visible online and can distort our picture of ordinary fans. The broader parasocial literature is large and heterogeneous; a 2024 review identified hundreds of studies across just a five-year publication window, reflecting how many different forms of parasocial experience now exist.9

So the scientifically useful boundary is not “parasocial relationship = unhealthy.” It is whether an emotional investment becomes rigid enough to override consent, privacy, ordinary functioning or other relationships.

07What would a decisive K-pop study actually test?

The biggest weakness in the current explanation is obvious: most evidence comes from general media figures, television characters, influencers or broad fandom samples—not controlled tests of idol dating announcements.

A strong experiment could fix this. Fans could first report parasocial relationship strength, fandom identification, romantic interest and perceived accessibility. They could then be randomly shown one of several matched updates about a fictionalized idol: a dating announcement, a non-romantic friendship announcement, a hiatus, or a neutral career update. Researchers could measure disappointment, jealousy, perceived norm violation, identity threat and willingness to disengage.

If the prediction-update account is right, distress should not be explained by romantic interest alone. It should increase when the news violates a strong pre-existing model. If the identity account is right, fandom identification should predict reactions that extend beyond the dyad—for example, concern about group conflict or perceived threat to the community. If the attachment account is right, stronger parasocial relationship measures should predict greater loss-like affect across several forms of disruption, not only dating.

That study would let us stop treating “betrayal” as a single folk category and start decomposing it into measurable processes.

08The larger human question.

K-pop makes this phenomenon unusually visible, but the underlying problem is much broader. Humans continuously build models of people they do not know personally: politicians, streamers, athletes, fictional characters, podcast hosts—and increasingly, artificial agents.

The boundary between “real relationship” and “mere media” is therefore less psychologically clean than it looks. Reciprocity matters. Consent matters. Reality matters. But the human side of the bond can still contain genuine memory, expectation and emotion.

That is why idol dating can feel unexpectedly personal without being a real betrayal. The event can force a meaningful social model to change. The feeling is real; the relationship remains asymmetric; and neither fact cancels the other.
