Why Can’t We Stop Scrolling?

There is no single “dopamine button” in your phone. Repetitive scrolling is better understood as a control loop in which uncertain rewards, learned habits, personalization, boredom and low-friction interfaces make one more swipe easier than an explicit decision to stop.

You open a short-video app to answer one message. Twenty minutes later, you are watching a person restore a chair, then a dog wearing shoes, then a political argument, then a recipe you will never cook. None of the individual videos was important enough to justify the session. Yet stopping somehow required more effort than continuing.

The popular explanation is that your brain is “addicted to dopamine.” It is memorable—and scientifically inadequate. Dopamine is involved in learning and motivation, but repetitive scrolling is not a behavior that can be reduced to one neurotransmitter, one reward center or one platform trick.

A better account separates several processes. Social rewards can shape behavior through reinforcement learning. Repetition can make behavior habitual. Short-form feeds create anticipation because the next item is unknown. Personalization changes the distribution of what appears next. Switching itself can paradoxically increase boredom. And interface design can remove natural moments at which a person would otherwise reconsider whether to continue.123

Evidence boundary. Some of the strongest computational evidence concerns posting rather than passive scrolling. HUMAN uses it to establish reward-learning and habit processes in social-media behavior, not to pretend that every swipe has already been fit by the same model.

01Reward learning is real—but “reward” is not the same as pleasure.

Lindström and colleagues analyzed more than one million social-media posts from 4,168 users across several platforms and fitted computational models derived from reinforcement-learning theory. Users adjusted the timing of future posts in ways consistent with learning from social rewards such as likes. An online experiment with 176 participants then manipulated reward rates and showed a causal effect on posting latency.1

This is unusually strong evidence that social-media behavior can follow reward-learning principles. But it is often misreported. The study did not show that scrolling is caused by “dopamine hits,” nor did it measure dopamine release every time a user looked at a post. It showed that behavior can adapt to experienced reward in a quantitatively predictable way.

That distinction matters because reinforcement learning is fundamentally about updating behavior from outcomes. A reward can be informational, social, surprising or simply better than expected. The mechanism does not require every item to be intensely pleasurable.

02Frequent social-media behavior is partly habitual.

A 2026 Nature Communications study extended this computational account. Turner and colleagues modeled real-world posting data from 2,696 Twitter/X users, including a preregistered replication. A hybrid model combining reinforcement learning with a habitual process explained behavior better than either process alone. More frequent posters showed stronger signatures of habitual responding.2

Habit is important because it changes what “why did you do that?” means. Early in learning, a person may repeatedly check an app because doing so often leads to something valued. With repetition, the cue–action relationship itself can strengthen. Opening the phone during a pause, swiping when a clip ends or checking after a notification can become less dependent on a fresh calculation of value each time.

This helps explain a familiar experience: continuing even when the feed is not particularly good. If every swipe required a deliberate judgment—“Is another video worth my time?”—many sessions would probably end earlier. Habit allows the motor sequence to continue while reflective valuation lags behind.

03The next swipe is a bet on an unknown item.

Short-form video feeds add a distinctive ingredient: uncertainty. You cannot fully know what the next swipe will reveal. Sometimes the next item is dull; sometimes it is exactly what you wanted; sometimes it is unexpectedly compelling. This means that continuing is partly an information-sampling decision.

Wang, Weng and Zhou examined this directly in a 2026 study of compulsive scrolling on short-form video apps. Their survey-based model treated anticipation of upcoming videos as a partial-reinforcement mechanism. Greater anticipation was associated with more compulsive scrolling, while the interaction between content type and recommendation systems showed a more mixed pattern.3

The important limitation is design: this was not a randomized experiment that manipulated uncertainty and proved it causes scrolling. The evidence is nevertheless more directly about scrolling than the posting studies, and it highlights a psychologically plausible property of the feed: a mediocre item does not necessarily signal “stop.” It may instead generate the thought that the next one could be better.

04Personalization changes the stream you are learning from.

A recommender system does not merely choose content for a passive user. The user’s behavior also changes the future environment. Watch time, swipes, replays and other interactions can help determine what appears next. In other words, your actions modify the distribution from which your next sample is drawn.

An fMRI study by Su and colleagues compared personalized TikTok clips with generalized recommendations. Personalized videos produced greater activity in several regions, including components of the default mode network and ventral tegmental area, along with differences in functional coupling.4

This result is interesting, but it is not evidence for the headline “TikTok floods your brain with dopamine.” VTA activity in an fMRI contrast is not a direct measurement of dopamine release, and the study does not establish a unique addiction mechanism. What it does show is that personally selected content produces a measurably different neural response from generic content.

Personalization therefore matters less as a magical “hook” than as an adaptive environment: the better the system estimates what is relevant to you, the more often the next sample has a chance to exceed the value of stopping.

05We scroll to escape boredom—and switching can make boredom worse.

One of the most elegant pieces of evidence comes from research on digital switching rather than social media addiction. Tam and Inzlicht ran seven experiments with 1,223 participants. People often believed that switching among or within videos would help them escape boredom. Experimentally increasing switching, however, made people more bored and reduced satisfaction, attention and meaning.5

This creates a self-reinforcing paradox. Boredom motivates a search for something more stimulating. Rapid switching fragments engagement, which can make the current experience feel less meaningful and more boring. The resulting boredom then supplies another reason to switch.

Sometimes we keep switching because we are bored—and become more bored because we keep switching.

This is different from reward learning and different again from habit. The same observed behavior—another swipe—can therefore arise from several latent states: seeking a better reward, executing a learned routine, escaping boredom or responding to a new cue.

06Notifications can reopen a loop you had already left.

Scrolling sessions do not begin only because we consciously decide to browse. External cues can reopen them. In a 2026 Computers in Human Behavior study, Fournier and colleagues used an ecologically oriented task to examine social-media notifications. Notifications produced a transient slowdown in cognitive processing lasting roughly seven seconds, with disruption attributed to a combination of salience, learned conditioning and relevance appraisal.6

Again, “attention hijacked” should not be interpreted as absolute mind control. Other experimental work finds that visual social-media cues are not necessarily more distracting than equally complex neutral cues under all conditions.7 Context, current goals and psychological state matter.

The broader point is simpler: a notification can create a fresh decision opportunity for the platform at exactly the moment your attention was allocated somewhere else.

07Stopping is partly an interface problem.

Books have pages. Episodes end. A bowl becomes empty. These are not merely physical facts; they are decision points. They create moments at which continuing requires a new action. An effectively endless feed removes many of those boundaries. The next item is already available, so continuation can occur before a reflective “Do I still want this?” judgment becomes explicit.

Direct causal evidence isolating infinite scroll itself is still thinner than popular commentary implies. But intervention research shows that reintroducing friction can matter. Mertens and colleagues ran a three-week randomized controlled trial with 70 iPhone users. The intervention displayed customizable full-screen reminders after users exceeded self-defined time thresholds, forcing an explicit choice to quit or continue. Participants in the intervention reduced daily time on their most problematic app by about 29 minutes relative to controls, although the primary measure of problematic social-media use did not significantly improve.8

That is an important pattern. A stop prompt can change time spent without “curing addiction.” Small design interventions can alter behavior by changing when decisions are made, even if deeper motivations remain intact.

08Why can one person stop after five minutes while another loses an hour?

Because there is no single scrolling mechanism, there should not be one universal user. Computational studies already reveal individual differences in reward-sensitive and habitual processes.12 Boredom, current goals, fatigue, loneliness, content preferences and the quality of recommendations can all change the value of continuing.

This is why screen time alone is a crude phenotype. Two people can each spend an hour on a platform while doing psychologically different things. One may be deliberately watching educational videos. Another may repeatedly sample low-value content while wanting to stop. The second pattern is much closer to what “compulsive scrolling” is trying to capture.

09What experiment would actually test the scrolling loop?

A clean experiment would separate interface from content. Participants could view the same short-video pool under three randomized interfaces: continuous infinite scroll, discrete pages that require an explicit “continue” action, and continuous scroll interrupted by periodic self-chosen stop prompts. The recommendation quality and video sequence would be held constant.

Researchers could then model swipe latency, stopping probability, re-entry after prompts, boredom, recall and subjective value. A computational model could estimate whether behavior is better explained by item-by-item value updating, a habit term, or both. If friction mainly affects the habit pathway, stop prompts should reduce continuation even when reported content value remains unchanged. If uncertainty is central, manipulating the predictability of upcoming content should independently alter sampling.

This type of design would move the field beyond broad claims about “addictive design” and identify which interface properties change which psychological process.

10So why can’t we stop scrolling?

Because “stop” is competing with several processes at once. Past rewards have taught us that checking can pay off. Repetition can turn checking and swiping into habits. The next item is uncertain enough to preserve anticipation. Personalization increases the chance that the environment remains relevant. Boredom encourages switching even when switching worsens boredom. Notifications can restart the sequence. And low-friction interfaces allow continuation without requiring a new commitment.

None of these processes makes a user powerless. But together they explain why willpower is a misleading unit of analysis. The more useful question is how a person’s learning history, current state and interface architecture interact to determine whether the next swipe becomes the last one—or merely another sample.
